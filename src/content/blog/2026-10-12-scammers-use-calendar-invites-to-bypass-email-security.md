You open your digital calendar to plan your day and see an entry for a meeting you do not remember scheduling. The notification might claim you have a new voicemail, a pending software renewal, or a payment receipt for an amount like $298.99.

If you click the link in that entry to investigate, you may land on a fake login page designed to steal your credentials. This is a growing form of phishing (a scam where attackers trick people into revealing sensitive information) that uses calendar invitations to bypass traditional email security.

## What happened

Security researchers have observed an exponential growth in calendar-based phishing attacks, according to Luke Wescott, a threat detection engineer at Sublime Security. These scams target both personal and work email addresses by sending calendar requests that appear directly in an individual's schedule.

The attack is effective because many calendar applications, such as Google Calendar, can automatically add invitations to a user's schedule without the user explicitly accepting them. This means a person might see a fraudulent entry even if they never open the original email.

The content of these invitations varies. Scammers may use titles like "PayPal unusual activity" or "Your auto-payment will be processed within 24 hours" to create a sense of urgency. Some attackers use legitimate platforms like Zoom to send these requests, which helps the invitations look more convincing to both people and security software.

Max Gannon, an intelligence analysis manager at the cybersecurity company Cofense, noted that these attacks are difficult to stop. He explained that filtering out invitations from legitimate platforms would also block real meeting requests, and even security tools powered by AI (artificial intelligence) struggle to identify them.

## Why it matters

The primary risk is the theft of login credentials. By luring victims to fake websites that mimic services like Google, Microsoft, or PayPal, attackers can capture usernames and passwords to access sensitive accounts or sell the data to others.

The scam also uses psychological pressure. An invitation might include a phone number instead of a link, directing users to call a fake "support" line to cancel a charge that does not actually exist.

Furthermore, these invites benefit from "borrowed credibility." Because the scam appears alongside legitimate appointments, such as a dentist visit or a weekly meeting with a manager, users are more likely to trust it. Attackers can even customise the invitations with company logos to make them look like internal communications.

## How this kind of attack works

This technique is a variation of traditional phishing, but it changes the delivery method. Instead of relying on you reading an email, the scammer places the trap directly into your daily schedule.

An attacker sends a calendar request to your email address, which your calendar app then automatically displays in your schedule. The event description contains a link to a fake website or a fraudulent phone number. When you click the link or call the number to resolve what looks like a legitimate issue, you are led to a site where you provide your password or personal information to the attacker.

By using the calendar, attackers bypass the step where a user might scrutinise a suspicious email in their inbox. The scam succeeds by exploiting the trust we place in our own organised schedules.

## What you can do

* **Adjust your calendar settings.** In Google Calendar, change your settings so that you only accept invitations from known senders or those you have manually approved.
* **Avoid clicking 'decline' on suspicious invites.** Clicking this button can signal to the attacker that your email address is active and being monitored.
* **Report and delete instead.** If you see an unexpected or suspicious entry, report it as spam or junk and then delete it.
* **Verify through official channels.** If a calendar entry claims there is a problem with a payment or a service, go directly to the official website of the service provider by typing the address into your browser rather than using the link provided.
* **Maintain a healthy level of suspicion.** Treat unexpected meeting invites with the same caution you would apply to an unexpected email, even if the invite appears to use your company's branding.

## The bigger picture

This trend shows that attackers are looking for new ways to bypass security filters by using legitimate services to deliver their messages. As scammers find ways to make fraudulent entries look like routine internal business, the distinction between a real meeting and a scam becomes harder to spot.

Watch for any unexpected entries in your digital schedule that demand immediate action regarding payments or account security.
