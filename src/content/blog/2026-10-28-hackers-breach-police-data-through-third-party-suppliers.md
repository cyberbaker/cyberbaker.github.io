In 2023, a ransomware attack—malicious software that locks files and demands payment to unlock them—on an identity card maker called Digital ID exposed the personal details of Metropolitan Police officers and staff. That same year, thousands of names and photos belonging to Greater Manchester Police officers were also stolen.

These incidents are part of a wider pattern. A report from His Majesty’s Inspectorate of Constabulary and Fire & Rescue Services has found that hackers have repeatedly breached third-party suppliers holding British police data since 2021. This means that even if a police force has strong security, the external companies they hire to manage their technology can become a way for criminals to reach sensitive information.

## What happened

The inspectorate, which oversees policing in England and Wales and fire services in England, found that the Police Digital Service and the National Police Chiefs’ Council shared information regarding multiple cyberattacks. These attacks targeted the ICT (information and communications technology) supply chains that hold police information.

While the report does not state exactly how many attacks occurred or which specific suppliers were hit, it provides several examples of the impact. In July, hackers stole the contact details of officers and staff from the Police National Legal Database, a service run by West Yorkshire Police used by every force in England and Wales. In September, Dyfed-Powys Police in Wales disclosed that a cyberattack had disrupted some of its non-emergency systems and potentially compromised staff information.

The Police Digital Service, which was established by the British government in 2021, recorded 252 cybersecurity incidents affecting policing in the first three months of this year. It also recorded 796 "near misses," which are incidents that did not result in a harmful breach of data or systems.

## Why it matters

The primary concern is that police data held by suppliers often sits outside the view of the national security operations centre. This makes it difficult for authorities to monitor who is accessing sensitive information. The risk is highest when these external companies handle data regarding victims, intelligence, investigations, or safeguarding.

For the public, this means that personal information held by the state may be at risk not because of a failure in a police station, but because of a vulnerability in a contractor's system. If an individual's data is part of a police investigation, that data could be exposed through these secondary links. 

Michelle Skeer, His Majesty’s Inspector of Constabulary, said: “The cyber threat to police forces and fire and rescue services is significant and expanding rapidly. A major cyberattack is not a hypothetical scenario.”

## How this kind of attack works

A supply chain attack does not target the main organisation directly. Instead, the attacker identifies a smaller, perhaps less secure, third-party company that has a contract to provide services to the main organisation.

The process usually begins with the attacker finding a weakness in the supplier's defences, such as poor password management or unpatched software. Once the attacker gains control of the supplier's system, they look for ways to move from that system into the networks of the organisations the supplier serves. 

In the context of policing, hackers target ICT suppliers—companies providing software, data storage, or identity services. Because these suppliers are already trusted to hold or transmit data, an attacker can use the supplier's legitimate access to bypass the main organisation's primary defences. This is effective because, as the report noted, many supplier contracts do not include ongoing requirements for cybersecurity checks, and the police often cannot see the technical logs that would show if a supplier has been breached.

## What you can do

If you are concerned that your data may have been caught up in a third-party breach involving a public service, you can take these specific steps:

* **Monitor for identity theft.** If you believe your personal details were involved in a breach, check your credit reports and bank statements regularly to ensure no one is using your identity to open accounts or take out loans.
* **Set up alerts for your personal information.** Use services that notify you if your email address or phone number appears in known data leaks, which can help you react quickly if your details are being traded online.
* **Update your security settings on sensitive accounts.** If you use online services for legal, financial, or official business, ensure they use multi-factor authentication—an extra layer of security like a code sent to your mobile phone—to prevent stolen data from being used to log in.
* **Report suspicious communications.** If you receive unexpected emails or texts that seem to be targeting you based on leaked information, do not click any links; instead, forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk or scam texts to 7726.

## The bigger picture

The report indicates that leaders in both the policing and fire sectors believe further attacks are highly likely. The inspectorate also criticised the government for leaving these sectors out of several key national security documents, including the Government Cyber Action Plan and the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill currently moving through Parliament.

As more public services move to external digital providers, the security of these third-party contracts will become a central issue for public safety. Watch for how the government addresses these gaps in the upcoming Cyber Security and Resilience Bill.
