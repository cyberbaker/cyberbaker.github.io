Sensitive personal records dating back to 1978 have been stolen following a cyberattack on the UK's Legal Aid Agency (LAA). While the agency initially believed the breach only affected those using its services since 2010, new forensic analysis shows the scope is much wider.

This means that even if you have not interacted with the justice system or used legal aid services for decades, your historical information may still be at risk. The theft of such old data demonstrates that personal information remains a target for criminals long after it was originally recorded.

## What happened

The LAA, an executive agency of the UK Ministry of Justice that manages the Public Defender Service, suffered a breach of its IT systems in April 2025. Criminals exploited vulnerabilities (weaknesses or flaws in software or system setups) within these systems to gain access and exfiltrate (copy and remove) data. 

The agency first disclosed the attack in May 2025, stating at the time that it likely affected people who had applied for legal aid since 2010. However, the agency has now confirmed that approximately 250,000 records predate 2007, with the earliest verified record dating back to 1978.

The stolen material is diverse. It includes names, dates of birth, National Insurance numbers, and financial details such as bank account information and property details. The breach also included probation data, such as nationalities and case reference numbers, as well as the names of judges, lawyers, and court reporters involved in Crown Court trials.

Hackers claimed to hold data on more than 2 million people and threatened to release it unless they were paid. Justice Minister Sarah Sackman described the incident as “significant and complex” in a statement to Parliament.

## Who is affected

The breach impacts several different groups of people and organisations:

* Applicants and recipients of legal aid.
* Individuals being supervised by the probation service.
* Legal aid providers, such as solicitors and barristers, whose contracts, office locations, and financial information were exposed.
* Judges, defence and prosecution lawyers, and court reporters involved in Crown Court trials.
* Opposing parties in certain civil cases.

## Why it matters

For the individuals involved, the theft of sensitive data like National Insurance numbers, equality monitoring codes, and financial assessments poses a long-term risk of identity theft. Because the stolen data includes historical records, the impact can surface many years after the original event. 

The agency noted that assessing the full extent of the damage is difficult because much of the stolen data was "unstructured, fragmented, or incomplete." This fragmentation can make it harder for individuals to know exactly which pieces of their personal history have been compromised.

For legal aid providers, the theft of LAA Online Portal credentials, such as names and email addresses, could lead to further targeted attacks on their professional accounts.

## How this kind of attack works

In this instance, the attackers did not necessarily need to guess passwords. Instead, they found vulnerabilities, which are essentially flaws in the software or the way the IT systems were configured. 

Once the criminals found these weak points, they were able to enter the systems. Once inside, the attackers moved through the network to locate high-value information. Instead of just looking at one file, they exfiltrated large amounts of data, essentially making copies of entire databases and moving them to their own servers. 

After the theft, the attackers attempted to use the stolen information for extortion. This is a process where criminals threaten to make private details public unless the organisation pays a ransom.

## What you can do

* **Monitor your credit reports.** Regularly checking your credit file can help you spot unauthorised applications for loans or credit that might indicate someone is using your stolen National Insurance number or identity.
* **Watch for identity theft related to sensitive codes.** If you have been affected, monitor for any misuse of your equality monitoring codes or financial assessment data in official correspondence.
* **Use multi-factor authentication.** Enable extra layers of security on your sensitive accounts, such as banking or email, to make it harder for criminals to use stolen credentials.
* **Be wary of unexpected communications.** If you receive unusual emails, texts, or calls asking for personal or financial details, treat them with suspicion.
* **Report suspicious activity.** If you believe you have been a victim of a scam, you can forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk.

## The bigger picture

The LAA breach is part of a period of heightened activity for cyber criminals in the UK. The government has responded by announcing a new cybersecurity strategy backed by £210 million to strengthen defences across the public sector. 

The Ministry of Justice has already begun retiring or isolating older, "legacy" systems that may carry higher risks. For those affected by this breach, it is wise to keep a close eye on your personal financial and identity information for any signs of unauthorised use.
