More than 300 instances of staff snooping through medical records have been reported this year. These reports involve employees using their professional access to view patient files without authorisation.

This matters because it breaks the fundamental trust between a patient and their healthcare provider. While many people focus on external hackers, these incidents show that personal data is also vulnerable to the people tasked with protecting it.

## What happened

The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO), which is Britain's privacy watchdog, has issued a warning to healthcare executives. On Thursday, the ICO confirmed it had written to healthcare leaders following a high volume of unauthorised access reports.

According to the ICO, there have been more than 300 reports of staff snooping through medical records so far this year. The regulator is now demanding that executives personally confirm they are taking sufficient steps to prevent unauthorised access to patient files. This requirement means that senior leaders must take individual responsibility for ensuring their organisations have the right safeguards in place.

The reports do not specify which healthcare organisations are involved, nor do they name the individual staff members. The exact methods used by staff to access these records have also not been disclosed. By writing directly to leadership, the ICO is moving to hold bosses accountable for the oversight of their staff and the security of the data they manage.

## Why it matters

When staff members access medical records without permission, it constitutes a significant breach of privacy. Medical data is highly sensitive, containing details about a person's health, history, and private life. 

For patients in the UK, these incidents can lead to a loss of confidence in the healthcare system. If people fear that their neighbours, colleagues, or acquaintances might look at their private information, they may become less honest with their doctors. This could ultimately impact the quality of care they receive if vital information is withheld due to privacy concerns.

For organisations, these breaches represent a failure in internal controls. It is not just a matter of individual misconduct; it is a signal that the systems meant to monitor and restrict access to sensitive information may not be working as intended. If an organisation cannot prevent its own staff from browsing files they have no business seeing, it suggests a weakness in their data governance.

## How this kind of attack works

In this context, the "attack" is not a technical exploit used by an outsider, but rather the misuse of legitimate access by an insider. Most healthcare organisations use electronic health record (EHR) systems, which are digital databases that store patient information.

Staff members, such as doctors, nurses, or administrators, are granted specific login credentials—a username and password—to enter these systems. These credentials allow them to view patient histories, test results, and contact details as part of their daily duties.

"Snooping" occurs when a staff member uses those valid credentials to search for a person's file for unauthorised reasons. Because the person has a legitimate reason to be in the system, their initial entry might not trigger an immediate alarm. They are not "breaking in" through a digital door; they are simply using their key to walk into a room they should not enter. 

This is why the ICO is pushing for executives to ensure they have active monitoring in place. Effective monitoring involves using software that flags when a user accesses a record that is not related to their current patients or their specific job role. Without this oversight, unauthorised access can go unnoticed for a long time.

## What you can do

While you cannot control how an organisation manages its internal security, you can take steps to understand your rights and monitor your own data.

* **Ask about data access policies.** Understanding how your provider monitors staff access can give you confidence that they take privacy seriously.
* **Monitor your medical records for discrepancies.** If you notice unusual activity or receive information you did not share, contact the organisation immediately.
* **Report suspected privacy breaches.** If you believe your data has been accessed inappropriately, you can contact the ICO to raise your concerns.
* **Check your privacy notices.** Healthcare providers are required to tell you how they use your data and how they protect it.

## The bigger picture

This crackdown by the ICO suggests a shift towards holding senior management personally responsible for data protection failures. Rather than treating snooping as isolated incidents of individual misconduct, the regulator is treating it as a systemic issue that requires executive oversight.

The focus is moving from the individual employee to the leadership responsible for the systems that allow such access to occur. We should watch to see if healthcare organisations respond by implementing more rigorous automated auditing and stricter access controls. Following the ICO's guidance will likely become a standard requirement for healthcare executives across the UK.
