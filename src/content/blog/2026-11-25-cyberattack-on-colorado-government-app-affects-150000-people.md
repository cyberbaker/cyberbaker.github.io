Nearly 150,000 people in Colorado may have had their personal information stolen following a cyberattack on a state-run mobile application. The breach targeted the myColorado app, a tool used by millions of residents for digital identification, storing documents, and accessing various government services.

This incident is significant because the app acts as a digital vault for sensitive information. When a central platform used for identity and official records is compromised, the stolen data can be used by criminals to impersonate individuals or attempt to access other private accounts.

## What happened

The Colorado Governor’s Office of Information Technology (OIT) discovered the breach on the evening of 18 November. An unauthorised user gained access to the data held on the myColorado app's platform. 

In response, OIT shut down the app to contain the incident and began an investigation. The app remained unavailable as of Tuesday afternoon. While the app has 2.1 million users, officials stated that the attack likely involved the theft of data tied to nearly 150,000 people. Investigators have not yet disclosed exactly what types of information were stolen by the cybercriminals.

The Gentlemen ransomware gang claimed responsibility for the attack on Sunday. This cybercriminal group uses ransomware (malicious software that locks a system and demands payment to release it). According to researchers at Halcyon, this group claimed 398 attacks during the last quarter, which surpassed the number of attacks claimed by all other ransomware gangs.

Sarah Tuneberg, the executive director of OIT, said: “We know how much Coloradans rely on these services, and we understand how this is frustrating and inconvenient. Protecting our users’ data is our first priority, and we will bring systems back online as quickly as we can once security is restored.”

## Why it matters

The primary concern for those affected is the potential for identity theft. Although investigators have not specified which data points were taken, the app is designed for digital ID and document storage, suggesting that highly personal details were at risk.

For people in the UK, this incident demonstrates the risks associated with the growing reliance on digital identity services. As more local authorities and government agencies move services to mobile apps, the concentration of personal data in a single application creates a significant target for attackers. If a single app holds your driving licence, tax information, or proof of address, a breach there provides criminals with the specific details they need to build a profile of your identity.

## How this kind of attack works

The breach occurred when an unauthorised user gained access to the platform's data. While the specific method of entry has not been disclosed, these types of attacks often involve finding a weakness in the software or using stolen credentials to enter a system.

In many modern ransomware attacks, the criminals do not simply lock the files. Instead, they often quietly copy or exfiltrate (transfer data from a computer or other device to another location) the information first. This allows them to steal the data and then demand payment to prevent them from releasing that information publicly. This approach gives the attackers more power during negotiations because they can threaten to leak sensitive details. In the case of the myColorado app, the attackers appear to have successfully accessed the data stored on the platform before the OIT could intervene and take the service offline.

## What you can do

If you use government or official apps to store sensitive documents, consider these steps:

* **Monitor your financial accounts.** Regularly checking your bank statements and credit reports helps you spot unauthorised activity early.
* **Be cautious of unexpected communications.** If you receive emails, texts, or calls asking for personal details, treat them with suspicion, as attackers often use stolen data to make their scams look more convincing.
* **Use physical backups for essential travel.** If you rely on a digital ID for travel, always carry a physical version of your documents to avoid being stranded if an app goes offline.
* **Report suspicious activity.** In the UK, you can forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk and scam texts to 7726.

## The bigger picture

The attack on Colorado is part of a wider trend of cyber incidents targeting public services. Recent reports show other organisations, such as the Queens Public Library in New York, have also faced ransomware attacks that disrupted essential digital services. Other agencies in Mississippi and California have also dealt with cyber incidents recently.

Watch for updates from your local authorities regarding the security of any digital services you use for official business.
