Hackers have built a library of more than 900 secretly recorded webcam videos to impersonate people during video calls. These recordings were stolen from victims while they waited for video chats to begin on fake meeting platforms.

This stolen footage allows attackers to create highly convincing appearances during follow-on scams. By using these recordings, they can make themselves look like a trusted colleague or friend during a video call.

## What happened

The US Department of Justice (DOJ) announced on Thursday that it has taken action against North Korean hacking groups involved in a social engineering scheme called FaceStealer. Social engineering is a tactic where attackers use psychological manipulation to trick people into revealing sensitive information or performing specific actions.

The FBI seized cloud infrastructure used in this campaign, which includes the library of over 900 stolen webcam videos. According to the FBI, the scheme has drained more than $400 million from the cryptocurrency and Web3 sectors over the last 18 months. The groups responsible are known as Sapphire Sleet, BlueNoroff, APT38, and Stardust Chollima.

Earlier this year, the hackers used a fake Microsoft Teams meeting to target the maintainer of a major open-source JavaScript library. By gaining access through this meeting, the attackers were able to create malicious dependencies, which are pieces of code that can be downloaded by others alongside legitimate software.

While these specific law enforcement actions were led by US agencies, the tactics used by these groups are global in nature and can affect anyone using digital communication tools.

## Why it matters

The sophistication of this attack means it can deceive even people who are highly experienced with technology. Patrick Grandy, an assistant director at the FBI’s Los Angeles field office, said the scheme is "capable of deceiving even the most experienced technical and security conscious computer user." He added that it exploits the trust we place in common video conferencing platforms and our personal professional relationships.

For organisations and individuals, the risk is that a video call from a known contact may no longer be a guarantee of their identity. Because each successful attack provides the hackers with new contact lists and video footage, the number of potential victims grows every time the scheme succeeds.

## How this kind of attack works

The attack begins when hackers hijack accounts on platforms such as Telegram, LinkedIn, Slack, or Discord. They use these hijacked accounts to send invitations to targets for a video meeting.

When the target clicks the link, they are directed to a typo-squatted domain. This is a website address that is spelled very similarly to a legitimate one, such as a slightly misspelled version of Zoom or Microsoft Teams, to trick the user into thinking they are in the right place.

The hackers host imposter versions of these meeting platforms on these fake domains. These interfaces are designed to look exactly like the real software. As the victim waits for the meeting to start, the fake platform secretly records them through their webcam.

Once the hackers have this video, they can use it to impersonate that victim in future attacks. They use the stolen footage to make themselves look like a trusted person during a video call, making it much easier to ask for money or sensitive data.

## What you can do

* **Verify unexpected requests through a different channel.** If a contact asks for money, cryptocurrency, or sensitive access during a video call, call them back on a known, trusted number or send a separate message via a different app to confirm it is actually them.
* **Check website addresses carefully.** Before entering any information or joining a meeting, look closely at the URL in your browser to ensure it is the exact, correct spelling of the service you intend to use.
* **Monitor your webcam activity.** Be aware of your webcam's indicator light; if it turns on when you are not actively in a video call, it could indicate that your camera is being accessed without your knowledge.
* **Use multi-factor authentication.** This adds an extra layer of security to your accounts, making it harder for hackers to hijack your profile to send fake invitations to your contacts.

## The bigger picture

The scale of these operations shows a significant evolution in how state-sponsored groups conduct financial theft. Justin Moore, a director of adversary operations at Arctic Wolf, noted that while tactics change, the goal remains exploiting trust to lower a target's guard.

As attackers move from simple phishing emails to complex video impersonation, the ability to verify identity through multiple steps becomes a necessary part of digital communication. Watch for an increase in highly personalised requests that appear to come from known professional associates.
