Scammers began posting fake deals as early as 1 September this year, more than a month earlier than they did last year. Many of these fraudulent posts do not even mention Black Friday, making them harder to spot using traditional methods.

This shift in timing and technique means that even if you are careful about checking web addresses for typos, you could still be targeted. These scams are designed to look like legitimate offers for services you likely already use.

## What happened

According to the security company Doppel, fake deals began appearing on 1 September. These scams often appear as posts within Facebook groups or on pages hosted via Google Sites, Blogger, or GitHub Pages. 

The attackers target a variety of sectors, including software, travel, payment services, and cryptocurrency. According to the research, business software has taken the top spot as a target in 2026, with travel, crypto, retail, and payment brands following.

Doppel found that half of the brand impersonation cases they tracked between September and late October were posted inside Facebook groups. Furthermore, 99.7% of the fraudulent ads they identified were run through Facebook Ads. 

The company noted that bargain and buy-and-sell groups are particularly effective because a scam post in such a group “carries the group’s implied vetting”. This means users may trust the post simply because it appears in a community they already use and trust.

## Why it matters

For individuals, the consequences can be immediate. More than a third of these fraudulent sites are built to steal logins for accounts that visitors already hold. This can give scammers access to personal data, financial accounts, or professional software.

For organisations, the risk is significant. Scammers are impersonating brands to steal customer credentials, which can damage a company's reputation. Even a brand that never runs sales can find itself at the centre of a Black Friday problem because the lure does not care whether the brand sells anything.

In the UK, where many people use social media to find deals, these sophisticated tactics can be deceptive. The use of legitimate hosting platforms and the delivery of impersonation texts through iMessage and RCS (a messaging standard used by many modern smartphones) means that standard SMS spam filters may not catch these messages.

## How this kind of attack works

These attacks rely on deception that bypasses the usual "red flags". Traditionally, people look for misspelled web addresses, but Doppel found that checking the address "passes most of this season's confirmed fake pages". Instead, scammers host their fake pages on reputable platforms like Google Sites, which makes the link appear more legitimate.

One common method is "cloaking". This is a technique where the website shows different content depending on who is looking at it. A security scanner, a brand employee, or a reviewer might see a completely harmless website, while a shopper actually looking for a deal sees the fraudulent lure.

Scammers also use conditional logic to hide their tracks. For example, one holiday sale scam was designed to show its checkout page only to people using a mobile phone. This makes it harder for desktop-based security tools to detect the fraud.

Attackers are also moving away from standard SMS to deliver impersonation texts. By using iMessage or RCS, they can often bypass the spam filters that typically monitor standard text messages.

## What you can do

* **Verify through official channels.** If you see a deal on social media, go directly to the company's official website or app by typing the address yourself rather than clicking a link.
* **Be wary of unusual offers.** If a discount on software, travel, or crypto seems unusually high, treat it with suspicion regardless of where you saw it.
* **Use multi-factor authentication (MFA).** Enabling an extra layer of security, such as a code sent to your phone, can prevent scammers from gaining access even if they manage to steal your login details.

## The bigger picture

The research shows that scammers are becoming more sophisticated by using legitimate infrastructure to hide their activities. They are moving away from obvious errors and instead using "cloaking" and reputable hosting services to blend in with real content.

Watch for scammers using established platforms to host their pages and bypassing traditional text message filters. Checking the official status of a sale through a trusted, independent source is a practical way to avoid these lures.
