Approximately 2.8 million living people have had their personal information stolen in a data breach affecting the Pentagon. This theft includes current and former U.S. military service members and staff, as well as their families.

If you or a family member are part of the military, this event may affect your personal privacy. The stolen data includes sensitive details that could be used for identity theft or targeted scams.

## What happened

Between October 2025 and mid-July 2026, several unauthorised users accessed the Defence Manpower Data Center (DMDC). The DMDC is a unit that maintains over 60 million records for U.S. military and civilian staff, as well as their families. These records help manage benefits like healthcare and retirement.

The attackers exploited a security vulnerability in an unspecified file-sharing system. According to a Pentagon official cited by CNN and Federal News Network, the breach affected about 2.8 million living people and nearly 300,000 deceased individuals.

The stolen data contained names, dates of birth, sex, race, military service information, and Social Security numbers. The DMDC stated in a notification that these personnel records were not encrypted (a process that encodes information so only authorised parties can read it). 

The Department of Defense, which oversees the DMDC, said it does not have any indication that the information has been misused. However, the identities of the hackers and whether they have communicated with U.S. officials remain unknown.

The DMDC also serves as a leading identity management provider. It links personnel to credentials like smart cards and passwords used to access Pentagon computer systems, buildings, and bases.

## Why it matters

The loss of this data creates long-term risks for the individuals involved. Because Social Security numbers and dates of birth were taken, victims are at a higher risk of identity theft. 

The DMDC manages records for a massive population. While there are 1.3 million active service members in the U.S. military as of March, the DMDC handles data for a much larger group of civilians and families. This means the scale of the potential impact reaches far beyond those currently in uniform.

The availability of such detailed personal profiles can also be used by bad actors to target specific individuals. This incident follows other recent thefts of federal workers' data, including a breach at the FBI in September. Such thefts can allow foreign entities to profile or target government employees.

## How this kind of attack works

The breach occurred because of a weakness in a file-sharing system, which is a tool used to move documents and data between different users or locations. 

In this instance, the attackers found a way to bypass the security controls meant to guard that system. Once they gained entry, they were able to move through the system to reach the personnel records stored within it. Because the attackers were able to remain inside the system for several months, they had a significant window of time to collect data.

A major factor in the severity of this breach was the lack of encryption on the files. Usually, even if a thief steals a digital file, they cannot read the contents without a specific digital key. Because these records were unencrypted, the attackers could read the names, Social Security numbers, and service histories as easily as if they were reading a standard, unprotected text document. This allowed them to extract the information without needing to break any further locks once they were inside.

## What you can do

* **Monitor your military and civilian benefits.** Check your healthcare and retirement accounts for any unauthorised changes or access, as these records were part of the stolen data.
* **Review your credit reports.** Regularly checking your credit helps you spot unauthorised accounts or loans opened in your name using your Social Security number.
* **Watch for highly personalised phishing attempts.** Be cautious of unexpected emails, calls, or texts that use your specific military history or personal details to seem legitimate, as these are often used to steal further information.
* **Use multi-factor authentication.** This adds a second layer of security, such as a code sent to your phone, which makes it much harder for someone to use stolen credentials to access your personal accounts.

## The bigger picture

This breach is part of a pattern of thefts involving federal workers' data. It mirrors the 2015 Office of Personnel Management breach, where more than 22 million U.S. government employees had their private records stolen. 

While the specific group responsible for the DMDC breach is unknown, the incident shows how vulnerabilities in secondary systems, like file-sharing tools, can lead to the loss of highly sensitive government data. Watch for further updates from the Department of Defense regarding how they plan to secure these records moving forward.
