A domestic abuse victim, who had relocated multiple times to escape physical and emotional abuse, had her new home address inadvertently shared with her abuser. This happened because a police force included the sensitive location in a legal document served to the perpetrator.

For most people, a data breach sounds like a stolen password or a leaked email address. However, when public services handle highly sensitive personally identifiable information (PII)—data that can uniquely identify an individual—the consequences can move from digital inconvenience to physical danger.

## What happened

In March 2024, Staffordshire Police shared the home address of a domestic abuse victim with her abuser, referred to in reports as "P". The error occurred when the force served a Domestic Violence Protection Notice (DVPN) to "P" following his release from prison. A DVPN is a legal notice used to protect victims by imposing certain restrictions on an individual.

The notice erroneously included the victim's current home address, a location that "P" did not previously know. The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO), the UK’s independent regulator for data protection, investigated the incident and issued a reprimand to the police force on 28 October.

The ICO found that Staffordshire Police failed to implement appropriate measures to prevent this disclosure. A contributing factor was the poor completion rates for mandatory training regarding data protection and the correct issuance of DVPNs. While the breach was serious, the ICO decided not to issue a fine, noting that the force had since taken steps to reduce future risks. These steps included introducing new technology, new internal procedures, and dedicated leadership roles for domestic abuse cases.

## Why it matters

This incident demonstrates that even organisations we trust to protect us can make errors that cause real-world harm. For victims of domestic abuse, the privacy of their location is a matter of physical safety. The ICO noted that because the victim had been forced to move numerous times to escape abuse, her address was highly sensitive information that required exceptional care.

For the general public, this case shows that data protection is not just about preventing identity theft; it is about safeguarding personal safety. When organisations handle sensitive data, they carry a responsibility to ensure their internal processes and staff training are sufficient to prevent such leaks.

## How this kind of error occurs

This type of breach is often the result of a failure in administrative processes rather than a malicious hack. It typically happens when an organisation uses a template to create a legal document. If that template is not configured correctly, it may automatically include extra information from a database—such as a home address—that is not legally required for the recipient to see.

A lack of rigorous checking can allow these errors to go unnoticed. If staff members have not completed up-to-date training, they may not recognise that they are about to disclose information that should remain confidential. Without secondary reviews or automated safeguards to flag sensitive data before it is sent, the incorrect document is issued, completing the breach.

## What you can do

If you are an individual or a small business owner handling sensitive information, you can adopt these practices to reduce the risk of accidental disclosure:

* **Review default templates.** Ensure that any forms or documents you use only include personally identifiable information (PII) when it is strictly necessary for the recipient to see it.
* **Double-check before sharing.** Always perform a final manual check of any data being transferred, altered, or disclosed to ensure no extra details have been included.
* **Tailor your training.** Ensure that any staff training is thorough and specifically covers the tasks your team actually carries out, especially when handling sensitive cases.
* **Verify contact information.** Regularly check that you are not inadvertently sending information to an old address, email, or phone number.

## The bigger picture

The ICO has previously noted that several organisations have been reprimanded in recent months for breaching sensitive personal data. This case shows a recurring challenge for public sector bodies: ensuring that administrative efficiency does not come at the cost of individual safety. Watch for whether organisations implement the structural changes, such as better training and improved technology, required to prevent these errors from recurring.
