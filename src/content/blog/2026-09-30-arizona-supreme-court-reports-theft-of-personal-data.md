Many people in Arizona may have had their private details stolen during a recent cyberattack on the state's court system. The Arizona Supreme Court announced that criminal hackers or their bots (automated software designed to perform tasks online) successfully copied personally identifiable information belonging to many residents.

This incident is significant because the theft of personal data often leads to long-term risks for the individuals involved. If your name, address, or other identifying details are part of this theft, you may face increased risks of identity theft or targeted scams in the coming months.

## What happened

On Friday, the Arizona Supreme Court announced that its court system had been targeted by an attack. According to Chief Justice Ann Scott Timmer, criminal hackers or their bots managed to copy personally identifiable information—data that can be used to distinguish or trace an individual's identity—belonging to many Arizonans.

The court's Administrative Office of the Courts is currently working to notify as many affected individuals as possible. While the investigation is ongoing, the court has not released the exact number of people whose data was taken, nor has it identified the specific group responsible for the attack. No hacking group has publicly taken credit for the incident.

A spokesperson for the court system told Recorded Future News that the attack did not involve ransomware (malicious software that locks an organisation's files and demands payment to unlock them). As of Monday, the hackers had not issued any ransom demands for the stolen data. Chief Justice Timmer stated that she spoke with the highest-ranking FBI leader in the state to coordinate the investigation and pledged to support efforts to minimise the risk to residents. The court is currently withholding further details to avoid impacting the ongoing investigation.

## Why it matters

The theft of personal data from a judicial system is a serious matter because courts hold vast amounts of sensitive information. When this data is copied, it can be used by criminals to impersonate individuals or gain access to other private accounts.

For the people affected, the risk is not just a one-time event but a long-term concern. Stolen information can be sold on various marketplaces, where it can be used for months or even years to conduct fraud. Even if this specific incident did not involve a ransom demand, the loss of privacy can lead to sophisticated phishing attempts, where criminals use stolen details to make fraudulent communications look legitimate and convincing.

## How this kind of attack works

In this incident, the court identified that the attack was carried out by criminal hackers or their bots. A bot is a piece of software that runs automated tasks over the internet. In a cyberattack, these are often used to scan for weaknesses in a network or to perform tasks at a speed a human could not match.

Once the attackers gained access to the network, they did not lock the files or demand money to return them. Instead, they focused on copying sensitive files. This process of unauthorised data transfer is often called data exfiltration.

Rather than using ransomware to disrupt the court's daily operations, the attackers focused on the quiet removal of information. Once the data is copied, the attackers can study the information, use it to launch further attacks against specific individuals, or sell the data to other criminal groups. This method allows the stolen information to be used as a tool for secondary crimes, such as identity theft or financial fraud, long after the initial breach has occurred.

## What you can do

* **Monitor your credit reports.** Regularly checking your credit files can help you spot unauthorised accounts or changes that suggest someone is using your identity.
* **Watch for unexpected communications.** Be cautious of any emails, texts, or calls that ask for personal details, even if they seem to come from a trusted source.
* **Set up alerts on your financial accounts.** Many banks allow you to receive notifications for any transaction or change in account settings, which can provide an early warning of fraud.
* **Use multi-factor authentication.** Enabling this extra layer of security on your important accounts makes it harder for criminals to use stolen information to gain access.
* **Wait for official notice.** The Supreme Court’s Administrative Office of the Courts is in the process of alerting people whose information it believes was copied.

## The bigger picture

Court systems are frequent targets for cyberattacks because they act as central repositories for sensitive personal and law enforcement data. Recent years have seen various courts across the United States, including in Kansas, Pennsylvania, and Georgia, suffer from ransomware and data breaches. For example, a ransomware attack in 2023 shut down nearly all of Kansas’ court systems, and a breach in Pennsylvania forced courts to provide time extensions for certain legal cases.

The investigation in Arizona will likely focus on determining the exact scope of the data stolen and identifying the perpetrators. If you live in Arizona, look for direct communication from the Supreme Court’s Administrative Office of the Courts to confirm if your specific information was involved.
