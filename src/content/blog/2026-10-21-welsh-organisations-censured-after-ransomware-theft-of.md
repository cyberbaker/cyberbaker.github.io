The personal data of 3,300 individuals, most of whom are children, has been stolen following a ransomware attack on two Welsh organisations. The breach has led to formal reprimands for the companies involved.

When the private details of children are compromised, the impact can last for years, affecting their privacy and security long into adulthood. This is because certain pieces of information, such as a full name or date of birth, are permanent and cannot be easily changed if they are misused.

## What happened

The Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO), the UK's independent body set up to uphold information rights, has censured two organisations based in Wales. These organisations are Ogi Networks Limited (ONL) and the Local Government Data Unit – Wales, which is known as Data Cymru.

The ICO issued these reprimands following a ransomware attack (malicious software that encrypts a victim's files and demands a ransom to restore access) that resulted in the theft of personal data. While the specific nature of the stolen data has not been disclosed, the ICO confirmed that the vast majority of the 3,300 affected individuals were children.

The identity of the attackers responsible for the theft remains unknown. The ICO's decision to censure the two organisations follows the incident, marking a formal reprimand for their handling of the situation.

## Why it matters

For the individuals and families in Wales affected by this breach, the consequences involve long-term risks. When data belonging to children is stolen, it creates a risk of identity theft or targeted scams. Unlike a stolen credit card, which can be cancelled, a person's name, date of birth, or other identifying details are difficult to replace.

For organisations, this incident demonstrates that data breaches carry regulatory consequences. Being censured by the ICO is a formal mark against a company's record. For small businesses and public sector bodies in the UK, this shows that even if a breach does not result in a financial fine, the regulatory scrutiny and the impact on public trust can be significant.

## How this kind of attack works

A ransomware attack often begins when an attacker finds a way into an organisation's network. While the specific method used in this case has not been confirmed, these attacks often involve gaining access through compromised credentials.

One common method is a brute force attack, where hackers use automated software to try thousands of different password combinations every second. They target predictable terms or common patterns until the software guesses the correct one. Another method is credential stuffing, which involves using lists of usernames and passwords stolen from other previous data breaches to see if they work on a new system.

Once an attacker has gained access, they move through the network to find sensitive information. In many modern ransomware attacks, the criminals do not just lock the files to demand money; they also steal copies of the data first. This process of moving data from a victim's system to a location controlled by the attacker is called exfiltration. By stealing the data before the attack is stopped, the criminals gain extra leverage, as they can threaten to release the private information publicly if the organisation refuses to pay the ransom.

## What you can do

* **Use a password manager.** These tools generate and store long, complex, and unique passwords for every account so you do not have to remember them.
* **Enable multi-factor authentication (MFA).** This adds a second layer of security, such as a code sent to your mobile phone, which makes stolen passwords much less useful to an attacker.
* **Avoid predictable patterns.** Do not use common words, names, or simple sequences in your passwords.
* **Update software immediately.** Keeping your operating systems and applications up to date ensures that known security holes are patched.
* **Report suspicious activity.** If you believe you have been targeted by a scam, you can forward suspicious emails to report@phishing.gov.uk or scam texts to 7726.

## The bigger picture

The theft of data belonging to children is a serious issue that requires organisations to take data protection seriously. As attackers continue to target entities that hold large amounts of personal information, the focus remains on finding ways to bypass security through weak points in an organisation's defences. 

Watch for updates from the ICO regarding how they handle similar cases involving the protection of vulnerable individuals.
